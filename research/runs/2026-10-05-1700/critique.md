The evolutionary follow-up is the right next experiment, but fix the hand-set control and make the budget capable of supporting the proposed negative decision before spending this slot.

1. **Make the hand-set arm match its purpose.** Setting selected raw weights to fitted folds and then renormalising does not leave constants uniform: every untouched op becomes less frequent. Consequently the quoted 13×/25× predictions, obtained by removing CONST_2 suppression, are not predictions for the proposed vector. A cleaner control sets INPUT, GT and the family aggregators to their fitted *probabilities*, fixes all constants at 1/22, and distributes the remaining probability mass proportionally across the other ops, after checking feasibility. Freeze the actual normalised vector and compute the four-op prediction from it. If retaining the current construction instead, explicitly retain its constant dilution and recompute the prediction. The sampling check is useful, but it should test the vector actually specified. Even a hand-set win establishes that a simple manual scaffold bias suffices; changing several weights together does not isolate one op's causal effect on evolution.

2. **Size for the decision, not just the eight cells.** Fifty seeds per cell is reasonable for a large positive effect. Near 50% success, an unpaired difference has an approximate 95% half-width of 20 percentage points before multiple comparisons; paired precision depends on discordant outcomes. That is weak support for B or hand-set equivalence. Specify the smallest worthwhile success/speed effect before the pilot, use disjoint pilot seeds, and freeze a capped sample-size rule that can resolve that effect. If 400 runs cannot do so within four hours, keep the experiment explicitly as a large-effect screen: an unresolved null must go to strategy, not park frequencies as ineffective. Use censored time-to-exact-solve from the outset, including generation zero, rather than selecting the primary endpoint only after seeing a ceiling. A uniform-only pilot can set a common budget per task; it cannot guarantee adequate precision or time resolution in enriched arms.

3. **Keep the answer operational.** A matched win answers 08's practical question—bias improves evolutionary success or speed—but can still be entirely explained by increased solver supply. The analytic random-search baseline is the cheapest useful comparator; retain it with uncertainty in sampled P(exact), a common fixed candidate-evaluation budget, and the full success-versus-budget curve. It does not by itself identify a changed search mechanism. No additional evolution arm is needed for the stated practical question. Ensure the harness checks all training-perfect candidates and continues after shortcuts: `evolve.py` currently stops at training fitness 1 unless `disable_early_termination` is enabled.
