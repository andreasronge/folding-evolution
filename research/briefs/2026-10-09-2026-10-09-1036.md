# Brief: run 2026-10-09-1036. Do learned fragments still help on compositions they were not taken from? (root 10, question 32) — yes, F/C 1.47×, F/W 1.20×

**What was tested and why.** Run 0843 showed that learned solver fragments, inserted as block
edits, speed up search 1.57× beyond the fitted table C on the cells they came from. Those
fragments were mostly the bank's shared 3-token syntax, so the real test was whether they help
on compositions they were not taken from. We froze each corpus's whole library (32 fragments
from all four training cells) and compared three arms on C's unchanged search:
- F: fragment block edits.
- W: blocks sampled from C's own chain, a library-free control.
- C: no block edits.

The primary bank was then-addition (`A>B ? C+D : E`). The 8 protected comparison-gate holdouts
served as a reference. The question it answers: is a separate fragment repertoire worth trying
to acquire, or does the cheaper W edit do the job?

**What happened.** The run is complete and valid: 15 360 searches, 152 min in the queue, and
every rebuild, replay and audit passed. 95% intervals over 16 corpora. On then-addition:
- **F/C 1.47× [1.38, 1.56]**, 16/16 corpora.
- **F/W 1.20× [1.11, 1.29]**, 14/16 corpora; 1.15× on pairs where both arms solved.
- W/C 1.23× [1.15, 1.31].
- Solve rates: C 87.1%, W 88.9%, F 90.8%.

On the holdouts: F/C 1.75×, F/W 1.27×, W/C 1.38×.

F/C's change from the training cells is 0.93× [0.83, 1.05], unresolved. By contrast, C's own
advantage over a token fit lost a third across the same shape change. Pre-registered rule 2
fired: *repertoire earns acquisition review*. My guesses for F/C and W/C were right; F/W came in
a little higher than I expected.

**What we learned.** The fragment gain largely held on compositions the libraries were not
taken from, and fragments still beat library-free chain blocks by about 1.2×. That makes a
repertoire a candidate target for acquisition, not just a training-cell artefact. The F/W gain
only marginally clears the 1.10× bar we called worthwhile, so "worthwhile" is not established.

suggestions to the strategist:
1. **Acquisition cost.** The solver corpus cost about 48 000 worker-seconds, which F's saving
   over W repays only after roughly 96 000 searches. The step toward acquisition is to build
   libraries from cheaper sources and score them against W: pre-solve parent tapes, which
   already exist from 27/28, or a few solvers per cell. Within-run harvesting is the endogenous
   version, but it needs new engine work.
2. **Why W beats C** (cheaper, about 75 queue min). W/C is 1.2–1.4× on every bank and needs no
   library.

If neither earns a slot, ending the run on this clean result is reasonable. 11 of 40
experiments are used, and about 18 h remain.

**Failures and uncertainty.** No failures. Limits:
- Both banks are development banks. The libraries are the shared syntax that then-addition
  needs by construction. So this is reuse of one externally fitted procedure across one shape
  change, not modularity or fresh-bank transfer.
- There was no supply control on this shape.
- The gain is uneven across cells: F/W ranges 0.96–1.54×, with only 5/16 cells resolved.
- Nothing was acquired by evolution.
