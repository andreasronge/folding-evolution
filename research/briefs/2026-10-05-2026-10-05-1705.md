# Brief: run 2026-10-05-1705, does a fitted op bias speed evolution? (08)

**What was tested and why.** Run 1558 showed that an `op_weights` vector fitted on sum>1/5
(or max>1/5) makes exact solvers of the held-out sum>2 (max>2) 5–9× more common in random
sampling. This run asked whether evolution also gets faster (A), or only sampling does (B,
what findings item 12 predicts). Four frozen vectors per task: uniform, matched fit, the other
family's fit (mismatched), and a hand-set scaffold (INPUT, GT and the aggregator raised,
constants uniform). Endpoint: evaluations to the first exact solve, paired seeds, two looks.

**What happened.** 650 runs in 20 min (of 8 h), complete; all 642 solvers re-verified exact;
8 runs censored. Pre-registered outcome: **Partial**.

| Ratio of median evaluations | sum>2 | max>2 | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| uniform ÷ matched | **4.33** (2.60–6.92) | **3.58** (1.91–6.00) | faster |
| mismatched ÷ matched | 1.66 (0.96–2.34) | 1.80 (1.23–2.80) | unresolved |
| matched ÷ hand-set | 0.93 | 1.08 | no difference |
| uniform ÷ mismatched (unregistered) | 3.26 | 1.93 | — |

- The mismatched vector has no sampling lift on these tasks (1.02×, 1.33×), yet it is 2–3×
  faster than uniform.
- The product model from 1558 failed out of sample. It predicted 17× / 22× sampling lift for
  the hand-set vector; the measured lift was 5.45× / 11.07×.
- Every arm beat computed random search by 9–40×.

**What we learned.** A fitted frequency bias does speed evolution on an unseen family member
(about 4×), so B is out. But most of the gain is generic: the wrong family's vector gets most
of it, a hand-set scaffold gets all of it, and sampling lift does not predict evolution speed.

  fitted vector per family, one evolution setup.
